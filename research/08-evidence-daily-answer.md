# 08 — Do older people answer a daily prompt from family? Published evidence

Date: 13 September 2026. Method: 30+ web searches and 60+ fetches of company filings, press releases, government reports and peer-reviewed papers. Numbers are quoted as published; where a figure is derived, an estimate, or from a secondary source, it is marked. No number below is invented. Rows 43–49 were added in a same-day supplementary pass that independently re-ran the search and cross-checked several figures already above (e.g. row 18's Jeongeup CareCall study), which held up.

## 1. Answer in five lines

1. No family-presence product (Famileo, GrandPad, Tinybeans, Mitene, Storyworth, Chikaku, Snug) publishes a per-day answer rate for the older person. The only hard per-prompt rates come from research on daily prompts to 70+ adults: **~78–82% of daily prompts answered in the first week**, declining slowly (about 1.5 points a week in older adults) — so plan for **65–75% of days answered by week 12** without extra motivation.
2. Vendor "response rates" are cumulative or "ever-answered" figures (Naver CareCall "96% respond"); in an independent sample the same service completed only **~3.4 of ~13 scheduled weekly calls per person** over three months, and calls averaged 29 seconds. Treat any headline above 90% as not a daily rate.
3. Morning is the right time (evening prompts get 18% fewer answers), the older person is not the weak link (their compliance decays half as fast as younger adults'), and the two biggest predictors of answering are **owning a smartphone (odds ×4.4)** and **being younger (−4% odds per year of age)**.
4. Older adults prefer a human voice, but answer asynchronous prompts more reliably than live calls (75+: 64% vs 27% response); automated check-ins are rated "helpful" by fewer than half and are described as "not care". A real person's question delivered asynchronously is the combination the evidence favours.
5. What keeps them answering: grandchildren content, a reply coming back (reciprocity), and a family cadence that is capped near one item a day (Famileo's 28 messages per 4 weeks). What stops them: hospitalisation/institutionalisation (the main churn for elder devices), physical limits (vision, fingers), and 30–40% of invited 65+ refusing any "monitoring"-framed product at enrolment.

## 3. What it means for Vela's design

**Expected answer rate.** The closest published analogues to "one short prompt a day, answered by tap or voice" are EMA and automated daily-survey studies in 70+ adults. They give 77–86% of prompts answered in week one (rows 22, 23, 25), with a slow linear decay of roughly 1.5 points a week in older adults (row 24). Plan the product and the pilot around **~80% of days answered in weeks 1–4 and 65–75% by week 12**; anything above 90% sustained would be exceptional. These studies paid participants and had no family in the loop, so Vela can hope for better, but should not promise it. A closer real-world analogue — Mago-Channel, a TV-connected family photo/video service tested with 115 Japanese older adults (mean age 74.3) — found ≥1 access per day from week two onward and 92.2% still engaged at three months (row 43), which corroborates the EMA-derived estimate even though that service is receive-and-view, not Vela's daily ask-and-answer loop. The Korean AI-call data is the cautionary tale: a vendor "96% respond" (row 16) sits beside an independent ~25% per-scheduled-call completion (row 18) and an 88% "call success" (row 19). Vela's own metrics must be per-day and per-person from the start.

**The light and the quiet notice must tolerate missed days.** With one in four or five days unanswered even among willing participants, a notice on the first missed day would fire 5–7 times a month per parent. Use the pattern of the check-in services that have survived: Iamfine retries five times over an hour before alerting (row 14); Enrich waits 24 hours, then phones the person, then the family (row 15). Vela should treat "no answer by usual time" as a soft state (light dims), escalate only after a re-prompt and a grace window, and learn each parent's usual answer window rather than using a fixed hour.

**Morning is right; smartphone is the gate.** Evening prompts get 18% fewer answers (row 22); the morning ask is correct. Smartphone ownership multiplied the odds of answering by 4.4 and every year of age cut them by 4% (row 22); among the frail 85+, only 19% could initiate contact themselves (row 30). Vela Light on the parent's own smartphone will work for the 70–80 segment; for the 80+ non-smartphone parent it needs a fallback that requires no initiation from her (auto-answered device, or a phone call that plays the family's voice and takes a keypress).

**A real person, delivered asynchronously.** Older adults say they prefer a human voice and a phone call (rows 19, 30, 33), and automated check-ins are rated helpful by under half and described as "not care" (row 19). Yet 75+ adults actually respond twice as often to an asynchronous channel as to a live automated call (row 27), and daily automated prompts had only 1.65% withdrawal (row 25). Vela's "ask" from a named family member, in their voice, answered when she is ready, is the combination the evidence supports: the social presence of a person with the answer rate of an asynchronous prompt. Do not automate the ask itself; automate only the reminder and the fallback.

**What sustains answering.** (a) Grandchildren: the GrandPad diary users who kept going were the ones receiving grandchildren photos (row 5); grandparents in WhatsApp groups mostly react to grandchildren content (row 37); Lindley's elders found direct contact with grandchildren "a rarity" (row 33), so Vela's word-to-teach and story-prompt asks supply something they cannot get otherwise. (b) Reciprocity: family asymmetry is the norm — elders give more (row 33). The reply she hears next morning is not a feature, it is the retention mechanism; measure reply rate as closely as answer rate. (c) Cadence: Famileo caps family posting at 28 messages per 4 weeks (row 2) and still runs 260,000 families largely on word of mouth (row 1); Tinybeans' paying parents show DAU/WAU of 61% (row 6). One ask a day is the proven family-side rhythm; the family, not the parent, is the side most likely to lapse, so Vela's product should nudge the adult child and grandchildren when the queue of asks runs dry (Famileo's "deadline" mechanic).

**Enrolment and churn.** Expect 30–40% of invited 65+ to refuse anything framed as monitoring (row 31: 31% declined, 35% dropped; other trials 42.5% refusal). Vela should be introduced as a family thread, with the light as a by-product, and consent language should say what the family sees. The dominant churn for elder devices is not boredom but hospitalisation and moves into care (rows 11, 32: 43% loss in 12 months at mean age 85). Physical limits (fingers, eyesight) cause fast early quits (row 5): the one-tap answer must be large, and voice must work without finding a button.

**Loneliness claims.** Only human contact has trial evidence: lay callers twice a week for four weeks (d = 0.48, row 28) and weekly 30-minute calls for 36 weeks (−16.6% UCLA, row 29). Video calls have very uncertain evidence (row 42). Vela can honestly claim to reduce family silence and increase contact frequency; it should not claim to treat loneliness until its own pilot measures UCLA-3 before and after.

**Benchmarks for the paid plan.** Monthly paid retention of 89–93% (≈7–11% monthly churn; Tinybeans, row 6) is what a family-memory subscription achieves at scale; use 90% monthly retention as the target for Vela Light and 70% word-of-mouth share (Famileo, row 1) as the growth model.

## 4. Gaps we still cannot answer from published data

1. **Per-day answer rate from family-prompt products is still mostly unpublished, with one partial exception.** Famileo does not publish whether the parent reads the gazette; Tinybeans, Mitene and Aura do not break out grandparent activity; Storyworth publishes no completion or weeks-answered figures; Chikaku's "very high" continuation (row 11) is unquantified. The one real quantitative exception is Mago-Channel (row 43): ≥1 access/day and 92.2% three-month completion, reassuringly close to the 80%→70% estimate borrowed from paid EMA research prompts — but it is a passive receive-and-view service with a small, self-selected sample (n=115) over only 3 months, not a daily ask-and-answer loop like Vela's.
2. **No study of voice replies from 70+.** Voice-note adoption is 47% among Boomers in one secondary survey (row 40) and unmeasured for 75+; no trial compares tap vs voice answer rates in this age group.
3. **No RCT of an asynchronous family prompt** (as opposed to live calls or AI calls) on answer rate, loneliness, or family contact frequency. The DFP and Wayve deployments are qualitative and tiny.
4. **No false-alarm data for "silence" notices.** Nobody publishes how often Snug, Iamfine, Enrich or the Japanese kettle/bulb services trigger a notice that turns out to be a missed tap rather than an incident, or how families respond.
5. **Country gaps.** All hard numbers are from Korea, Japan, the US, Belgium, Germany, Spain and France; nothing for Russian-speaking or Taiwanese families, where Vela's first pilots run.
6. **Human vs person-from-family.** The evidence compares humans with machines; none compares a stranger's prompt with a family member's. The reciprocity argument (row 33) is theory plus qualitative work, not a measured effect.

The pilot should therefore log, per parent and per day: prompt delivered, answered (tap/voice), time-to-answer, reply received, reply heard; plus UCLA-3 at week 0, 4 and 12. Those six numbers would close gaps 1, 2 and 4 for Vela's own population within one quarter.
